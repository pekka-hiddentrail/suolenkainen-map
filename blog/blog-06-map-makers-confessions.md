# Map-Maker's Confessions

*Somewhere behind every ruled and numbered thing there is, usually, just a person who liked drawing coastlines too much. That part of the story doesn't need a court or a card to explain it. It only needs telling.*

The passion for maps is a peculiar thing. Once it takes hold, it never really lets go. It sits quietly in the background while life gets on with itself, and then one day you find yourself cutting hexagons out of cardboard at the kitchen table and thinking: yes, this is exactly what I am supposed to be doing.

This project is, in many ways, a cashing-in of a lifelong obsession. The difference this time is that there's a structure, a system, and — for the first time — an audience.

## Graph Paper, Dungeons, and the Excel Years

It started the way it starts for a lot of people: role-playing games and a stubborn refusal to use anyone else's maps.

As a kid, maps came in all shapes and purposes. Dungeon maps scratched in pencil. Regional overviews with roads threading between hand-lettered settlements. The obligatory island continent with a mountain range in the middle and a suspiciously convenient port city on the southern coast. The usual.

When paper ran out of road, Excel appeared on the scene. And before you laugh — you can make an extraordinarily good grid in Excel. Street maps, dungeon layouts, anything that benefits from a clean, scalable grid becomes genuinely enjoyable to build in a spreadsheet. Somewhere on an old hard drive there are files from the late nineties with names like *Large Dungeon* and *Ruins*, sitting quietly in the digital equivalent of a shoebox.

## From Grids to Continents

Eventually the grid was not enough. Excel has a shape tool, and once that door opened, there was no closing it.

A free-form shape, dragged and nudged into the outline of a continent. Then height maps. Then roads. Then biomes, ocean currents, rainfall patterns, named mountain ranges, river systems that actually made hydrological sense. Nations with borders that reflected geography. Cultures shaped by those nations. Religions growing from those cultures.

The more that went in, the more was needed. And with the maps came the lore. Poems. Songs. Family trees stretching back eight generations for characters no one would ever meet. Dozens of pages of names, histories, and invented languages that will never be spoken aloud. The maps stopped being maps and became something else: a canvas for imagination, and a muse that never seemed to run dry.

## The Pile of Shame and the Plateau of Repetition

Here is where honesty is required.

Alongside the maps, there is a long trail of unfinished things. Paintings set aside mid-layer. A bass guitar that goes quiet for months at a time. Miniatures primed but unpainted, staring accusingly from a shelf. Anyone who has ever owned a grey pile of shame knows the specific guilt of it.

The pattern is consistent. A new project arrives and gets attacked with full enthusiasm — 110%, no compromise, all hours. And then comes what might as well be called the Plateau of Repetition: the part where novelty runs out and sheer grinding takes over. Painting the fifteenth identical miniature. Writing the same chord progression again. The moment where the work stops feeling like discovery and starts feeling like homework.

Two things reliably kill a project. The first is boredom — specifically, the absence of visible progress. The second is silence: working alone, in isolation, with no feedback loop to keep the energy alive.

This project is built to fight both.

## A Map That Is Always Finished

The wall display is not just a storage solution. It's a psychological one.

The map will live on the wall in its entirety at all times — five tiles, ten tiles, fifty tiles, it doesn't matter. At every stage it is a complete piece of art. Not a draft. Not a work in progress gathering dust. Something finished enough to look at, to appreciate, to show people. And yet always one session away from being different.

There is no plateau here, because every tile is its own small result. Every session produces something that was not there before. The map is always done, and it is never done, and that tension is exactly the point.

## Why This Time Feels Different

The silence problem is harder to solve than the boredom problem, but this time there's a system for that too.

The rules don't just govern the tiles. They govern the sharing. They tell me, periodically, to write something. To post. To ask for feedback. To reach out. This blog post exists because the dice said so, in a manner of speaking. The system makes community part of the process rather than an optional extra.

And at some point, I intend to reach out to Jerry Gretzinger directly — to thank him for the work that helped shape this system. His decades of dedication to a single, evolving map are part of what made this feel possible.

The rules will keep changing. The system is not finished and probably never will be. That is where you come in — ideas, suggestions, challenges to the logic, better solutions to things I have clearly overcomplicated. If you see something in the system that could be done differently or better, say so in the comments. I mean it.

## Two Hundred Blank Cards

There is one part of this project that already has the hallmarks of a future problem.

The card deck. Two hundred blank cards, cut and stacked, staring back from the desk with the quiet menace of a task that has not yet been started. Each one needs to become a proper, decorated, functional card. Each one is part of the system. Each one will eventually matter.

The saving grace is that the rules handle this too. Every so often, the dice send me to the deck. Not to finish it — just to do one card. One card at a time, on the sessions when the system says so. It might take years. That is fine. Or it will never quite be finished. That is also fine.

The deck, like the map, is always in progress and always complete.

## The Hexagons That Weren't

And now, the part of this post where I own up to something.

When I ordered the first batch of wooden tiles online, from a supplier outside Europe, I specified 15cm hexagons. What I got were 15cm hexagons — technically. One "15cm" was the flat-to-flat measurement across an edge. The other "15cm," the one I'd assumed matched, was measured corner to corner instead. The result is a batch of subtly stretched hexagons: interior angles that land somewhere between 115 and 125 degrees instead of a clean 120 across the board.

In the grand scheme of things, it's not a disaster. The tiles still tile. The magnets still line up well enough. But this first generation is a little wonky, and it will stay a little wonky, because I'm not about to redo a batch of tiles over a few degrees of drift. It's a good reminder of something I already half-knew from the two cardboard prototypes taped to my office wall: the early materials in a project like this aren't failures to be corrected, they're mementos of the version of the project that came before the version that counts. This batch joins that shelf, in spirit if not in fact — proof of a lesson learned rather than a mistake to hide.

For anyone else cutting their own hexagons: don't order a full batch of anything before you've confirmed exactly how the supplier is measuring it. Ask me how I know.

Going forward, the properly laser-cut tiles are coming from a Finnish supplier instead — full 120-degree angles, no ambiguity about which "15cm" is which, and the added bonus of supporting a local business and getting a material I can actually rely on session after session.

Like they say: you roll with the punches, and then you order the next batch more carefully.

## The Work Is Always Done

The way forward is simple, even if it isn't always easy: keep posting, keep making, keep collecting materials and ideas and comments from people who are paying attention.

The map on the wall will look different in a month. Different again in a year. And every version of it will be the right version for that moment.
