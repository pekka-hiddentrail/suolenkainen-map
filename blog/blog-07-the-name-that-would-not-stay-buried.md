# The Name That Would Not Stay Buried

## I. From Plan to Hand

*A plan is only ever a rumor about the future. The court can order weight, strangeness, stain, and silence at the border, but it cannot glue anything down itself. Someone still has to lift the actual scrap, choose the actual color, break the actual tool. What follows is what happened when the order finally reached a hand willing to carry it out — and, as always happens, the hand answered the order in a way the order didn't quite expect.*

The last post laid out the Surface plan for T001: a weighted, accumulated collage of electrical diagrams and art-supply drawings, stained yellow and orange, closed at the edges but for two seams left deliberately open. That was the instruction. This is what actually got made from it — and it turned out stranger and more literal than the plan on paper, in ways I didn't fully predict when I was still just listing rules.

## II. The Name That Refuses to Stay Buried

*Every newborn thing is given a name before it is given a face, and the court decreed this one's name must be spoken and then half-swallowed in the same breath. But a name does not always agree to disappear on command. It waited under the weight piled on top of it, and then it pushed back — not by breaking free, but by insisting on its own outline even from underneath everything laid over it.*

Cartography's anchor point — the mark every new tile gets, placed anywhere on the tile before it means anything — became a small scrap, roughly 3 by 8 centimeters, printed with the word "AIKA" in a plain technical font. It's Finnish for "time," and it sits middle-ish on the tile, exactly where an anchor point is supposed to go. Once it was placed, I did what the plan called for: covered it in scrap material, schematics fragments layered right over the top, following Surface's demand for a weighted, accumulated ground.

But this is where Attunement's Trace result actually took over the decision. Echo's Trace asked for one old mark that has to remain influential no matter what gets built on top of it, and I didn't want AIKA to just quietly vanish under the collage the way a buried thing usually would. So I went back in with markers and clarified its outline — the text started out plain white, easy to lose against the paper and print underneath it, so I redrew it in yellow instead, which reads far more insistently against the schematics scraps piled over it. It's buried, technically, the way Cartography's burial cost demanded of the tile's proclamation. But it's a burial that keeps arguing with itself.

## III. A Broken Tool Becomes the Terrain

*Somewhere in the gathering, an actual instrument gave itself over to the ground it once worked on. It did not come to make a mark in the usual way. It came to stop being a tool and start being terrain — fixed in place, half-covered, no longer able to lift or move or paint again on its own. What it leaves behind is not a picture of a brush. It is the brush.*

Surface's Substance result called for something strange — material that doesn't normally belong to a map, self-referential, tools becoming terrain rather than staying tools. The plan imagined this as drawings of art supplies. What actually happened went further: I epoxy-glued a real fan brush directly onto the tile and covered part of its stem with paper scraps, so it sits half-submerged in the same collage burying everything else. It's not a picture of a paintbrush anymore. It's an actual, physically fixed, permanently retired paintbrush, now doing the job of ground instead of the job of painting.

This is also where Attunement's restlessness finally gets its transfiguration. The whole-field instability that Pressure demanded — something unstable that had to become another force, material, or structure rather than just get filled in — turns out to be answered by exactly this: a tool that used to make marks becoming, instead, a fixed structural feature of the tile itself. The restlessness didn't get calmed. It got converted into an object.

## IV. A Streak Painted by the Tile Itself

*And where the tool once was, evidence remains of a single act it performed before it stopped moving forever — an orange trail, caught with yellow where the light of it seems to catch highest, laid down and left, as if the ground itself had reached for a color and then held perfectly still the instant after.*

Running from the fan brush is a streak of watercolor, mostly orange with yellow highlights, painted so it reads as if the brush itself laid it down in one last motion before being buried. This is Surface's stained Treatment in its most literal possible form — color as residue rather than decoration, the record of something that already happened rather than a choice made after the fact. It's also where Attunement's Omen finally lands: the hue drawn from the lion becomes, concretely, this orange-and-yellow streak — present, visible, and directly tied to the one object on the tile that looks like it could have made it.

## V. Two Edges Marked as Warnings

*Two mouths on the border were sealed once, and sealing was not the end of the matter. Now they are marked the way a hazard is marked: not opened, not silenced, but flagged so that no one mistakes them for ordinary, quiet edges. Something is going to want to cross there eventually. For now, the mark is only a warning.*

The north and northeast edges are the two that Cartography's Edge Inheritance left open and Surface's Opening asked to keep sealed rather than active. I marked both with a black-and-yellow checker pattern — the kind of visual shorthand usually used for hazards or caution — rather than leaving them blank or touching them with a faint wash the way the plan first suggested. It reads more like a warning label than a soft margin, and I think that's actually more honest to what those edges are: Origin's contagious pressure is sitting right behind them, waiting for a future neighbor to exist. Calling them "spread points" and marking them accordingly says plainly what the rules have been implying since the very first Cartography draw — something wants to leave this tile eventually, and these are the two places it's most likely to try.

## VI. What This Leaves for Inscription

What's physically true on T001 now: a name half-buried and half-insisting on itself at the center, a real broken paintbrush fixed permanently into the ground with an orange-and-yellow streak trailing from it, schematics and scrap layered around both, and two edges flagged with warning checkers rather than left quiet. Almost every obligation from Cartography and Attunement found a literal, physical answer here — the trace, the strangeness, the restlessness, the hue, the burial cost — which is more than I expected going in.

A word on "owe," since I keep using it: it's not the tile owing me anything, or one phase owing another. It's me owing the tile — or really, the map — whatever obligation the cards and the rules have placed on it and I haven't yet answered with my own hands. Cartography and Attunement hand down debts; I'm the one who has to pay them, eventually, in actual material.

What's still owed, in that sense: the technical buried mark that Cartography's burial cost asked for is arguably already present in AIKA itself, but I haven't decided yet whether Inscription treats that as satisfied or asks for something further, more explicitly technical, elsewhere on the tile. The Jules Verne sticker on its wooden scrap is still waiting off to the side, not yet placed. And the two warning-checkered edges are marked but not yet answered — they're a promise I've made to a neighbor that doesn't exist yet. Inscription is next, and it inherits a tile that's already more insistent about what it wants to say than I expected a foundation phase to leave behind.
